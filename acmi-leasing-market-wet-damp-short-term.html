ACMI Leasing Market 2026:
Wet, Damp and Short-Term Leases

ACMI flying fell in 2026 after two record years. How the market moved, how wet, damp and short-term leases differ, and what to agree before signing.

Nov 25, 2026 8 min read

TL;DR: The ACMI leasing market rents out aircraft with their crew, maintenance and insurance, paid by the block hour. After a record 2025, global ACMI flying to the end of Q3 2026 was more than 15% below the year before, according to ACC Aviation. Narrowbody demand fell hardest. Widebody demand rose 7%. A wet lease includes the full crew. A damp lease includes the cockpit crew only, and the airline supplies its own cabin crew. Short-term ACMI runs from a few days of AOG cover to a seasonal contract of up to seven months. Contracts are priced per block hour with a minimum hours guarantee, so the terms matter as much as the rate.

What is the ACMI leasing market?

The ACMI leasing market is where airlines rent capacity that arrives ready to fly. The lessor supplies the Aircraft, Crew, Maintenance and Insurance. The lessee pays per block hour and keeps the commercial side: its flight numbers, its schedule and its tickets.

The aircraft stays on the lessor’s air operator certificate (AOC). That is the defining point. The lessor dispatches the flight, rosters the crew and carries the safety responsibility, even when the aircraft wears the lessee’s livery. Our ACMI and wet lease service sources this capacity from AOC holders across Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

Europe is the largest ACMI region. It held about 51% of global ACMI block hours in 2025, according to ACC Aviation, while Africa and South America grew faster. The largest provider, Avia Solutions Group, states that it holds about 8% of passenger ACMI block hours.

How did the ACMI market change in 2026?

ACMI flying fell in 2026. ACC Aviation reported on 6 November 2026 that global ACMI activity to the end of Q3 was more than 15% below the same period of 2025. Summer 2024 was the peak for pricing. 2025 was the busiest year on record in block hours.

Three causes stand out: airport and airspace closures linked to the Middle East conflict, jet fuel about 70% dearer than in 2025 (IATA’s estimate), and later bookings. Airlines cut their own flying first. Wet-lease contracts were among the first lines to go.

Supply has thinned too. SmartLynx, a Latvian carrier that had flown ACMI and charter for more than 30 years, stopped flying in November 2025. Fewer providers means fewer spare aircraft when demand returns.

The medium-term case for ACMI still holds. The global order backlog passed 18,000 aircraft in May 2026, and deliveries remain below pre-pandemic rates. When airlines rebuild capacity, wet lease is the fastest way to add it. ACC expects airlines to stay cautious for the next 6 to 12 months.

What is the difference between a wet, damp and dry lease?

The difference is who supplies the crew and whose AOC the flight runs under. A wet lease includes a full crew. A damp lease includes the cockpit crew but not the cabin crew. A dry lease is the aircraft alone, flown on the lessee’s own AOC.

In every case the lessee keeps commercial control: it sells the seats, sets the schedule and uses its own flight numbers and traffic rights.

When does a damp lease make sense?

A damp lease fits an airline that has trained cabin crew but too few aircraft or pilots. Passengers see the airline’s own crew, service and announcements. The lessor still flies the aircraft under its AOC.

The UK Civil Aviation Authority, following ICAO, defines a damp lease as a wet-leased aircraft that includes a cockpit crew but not cabin attendants. The lessee’s cabin crew must first complete the lessor’s conversion and differences training, because they work under the lessor’s operations manual. Some regulators also cap the term. Jordan’s regulator, for example, limits a damp lease-in to 12 months.

The saving is the cabin crew cost: salaries, hotels and allowances for at least four cabin crew on a 180-seat Airbus A320, and six or more on a widebody. Recent examples include Estonian leisure carrier Marabu, which damp-leased two A320s from Bul Air for summer 2025, and Norse Atlantic’s 787-9 damp lease to IndiGo, which has since ended.

What is short-term ACMI, and how fast can it start?

Short-term ACMI covers anything from a single day to a single season. Providers sell it in three bands:

  1. Ad hoc and AOG cover, one day to two weeks. Replaces a grounded aircraft or a cancelled rotation. Some providers keep standby crews and can position an aircraft within hours of a confirmed request.
  2. Seasonal, one to seven months. Usually the northern summer (April to October) or a winter peak such as the year-end holidays. This is the core of the market.
  3. Long-term, 12 months or more. Often in the lessee’s livery, bridging a gap until delivery of new aircraft.

Regulation sets the outer limit in some markets. In the EU, an airline that wet-leases an aircraft registered outside the EU needs prior approval. For seasonal needs, that approval runs up to seven months and can be renewed once (Regulation 1008/2008, Article 13). Most national regulators require their own approval before a foreign-registered aircraft flies a domestic airline’s schedule. Build that time into the start date.

The A320 and the Boeing 737-800 are the types most often available at short notice. Both have large pools of type-rated crew, which newer types do not yet have.

How is ACMI priced, and what does the lessee still pay?

ACMI is priced per block hour, with a minimum hours guarantee (MGH) per month. If the lessee flies less, it pays for the minimum anyway. Avia Solutions Group lists exactly these terms for its ACMI work: a rate per block hour and a minimum hours guarantee.

The ACMI rate covers the aircraft, crew, maintenance and insurance. The lessee usually pays fuel, airport and navigation charges, ground handling, catering, crew hotels away from base and positioning flights on top. Rates by aircraft type are in our guide to ACMI costs per block hour.

In a soft market such as 2026, the minimum hours and the cancellation terms matter more than the headline rate. ACC Aviation’s advice is direct: do not assume a force majeure clause will protect a programme, and agree governing law and jurisdiction at the outset of any cross-border contract.

How should an airline or tour operator source ACMI capacity now?

Start with the mission, not the aircraft. Seats, sectors, block hours per month, base and start date decide which operators can bid. Then check five points before signing:

  1. The lessor’s AOC and registration, and your regulator’s position on foreign wet leases.
  2. The minimum hours guarantee, and what happens to it if you cut flying.
  3. Crew base, crew hotels and positioning, and who pays for each.
  4. Back-up cover: what the lessor provides if its own aircraft goes AOG.
  5. Termination, force majeure and governing law.

Group and tour programmes often sit between charter and ACMI. A single movement is usually a charter. A series over several weeks is often better value as a short ACMI. Our group and corporate charter desk quotes both when the pattern is unclear.
