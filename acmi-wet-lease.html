Flexible Capacity, When You Need It

ACMI (Aircraft, Crew, Maintenance, Insurance) — also called wet lease — is the structure of choice when an airline needs to add capacity quickly without the long-term commitment and regulatory overhead of aircraft acquisition. The lessor supplies the aircraft, flight and cabin crew, maintenance, and insurance; the lessee covers fuel, airport fees, and other operating costs, and flies the aircraft on their own schedule.

It sits at the full-service end of a leasing spectrum that also includes damp lease and dry lease, and is the standard answer to seasonal demand spikes, AOG recovery, route testing, and urgent freighter capacity.

Quick Answer

ACMI puts an aircraft, its flight and cabin crew, maintenance, and insurance under the lessee's schedule, while the lessee covers fuel, airport fees, and other operating costs. It sits at the "full-service" end of a leasing spectrum that also includes damp lease (lessee supplies cabin crew) and dry lease (lessee supplies everything except the aircraft itself, operating it on their own AOC).

ACMI terms typically run from a single flight up to around 18 months — far shorter than a dry lease, which usually runs 6 to 12 years — making it the go-to structure for seasonal capacity, route testing, and sudden operational gaps.

The Leasing Spectrum

"ACMI" and "wet lease" get used almost interchangeably, but the leasing world runs along a spectrum of how much the lessor provides versus the lessee.

The Practical Distinction

Wet and damp leases operate under the lessor's Air Operator's Certificate (AOC), which is what lets an airline add capacity almost immediately without regulatory delay. A dry lease requires the lessee to operate the aircraft on its own AOC, which takes considerably longer to arrange — a large part of why ACMI is the structure of choice when speed matters more than long-term cost efficiency.

Use Cases

Why Airlines Choose ACMI

Five scenarios where ACMI wet leasing is the right operational answer.

Seasonal Demand

Quickly add capacity during peak seasons without long-term fleet commitments — a classic example being airlines adding widebody capacity for the Hajj and Umrah pilgrimage season, when demand spikes sharply for a defined window.

AOG & Schedule Recovery

When one of your own aircraft is grounded (AOG) or a maintenance event disrupts your schedule, an ACMI lease can plug the gap almost immediately, keeping your network running while the underlying issue is resolved.

Urgent Capacity

Address sudden operational needs or unexpected demand spikes without the lead time a new aircraft purchase or dry lease would require. ACMI can often be arranged within days, depending on aircraft availability.

Market Testing

Launch new routes with minimal risk before committing to aircraft acquisition — if the route doesn't perform, you're not left holding a long-term asset. ACMI lets you prove demand before you commit capital.

Cargo & Freighter Capacity

ACMI isn't limited to passenger operations — freighter ACMI is a common way for cargo operators to add capacity during peak e-commerce or seasonal shipping demand without acquiring aircraft outright.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does ACMI stand for?

ACMI stands for Aircraft, Crew, Maintenance, and Insurance — the four elements the lessor provides under this arrangement, while the lessee covers fuel, airport fees, and other direct operating costs. It's also commonly called a wet lease.

What's the difference between ACMI/wet lease and a dry lease?

Under ACMI, the lessor provides the aircraft, crew, maintenance, and insurance, and the aircraft operates on the lessor's Air Operator's Certificate. Under a dry lease, the lessor provides only the aircraft, and the lessee operates it on its own AOC, supplying crew, maintenance, and insurance themselves.

How long does a typical ACMI lease last?

ACMI leases are generally short-term, ranging from a single flight up to around 18 months, making them suited to seasonal demand, route testing, or covering a temporary capacity gap. Dry leases, by comparison, typically run 6 to 12 years.

What is a damp lease, and how is it different from ACMI?

A damp lease sits between wet and dry: the lessor provides the aircraft, flight crew, maintenance, and insurance, but the lessee supplies its own cabin crew — often chosen by larger airlines that want more control over the in-flight passenger experience and brand consistency.

Who typically uses ACMI wet lease?

Airlines facing seasonal demand spikes, unexpected aircraft groundings (AOG), new route launches, or short-term capacity shortfalls are the most common users, alongside cargo operators needing additional freighter capacity during peak shipping periods.

Does the lessee need its own Air Operator's Certificate for an ACMI lease?

No. One of the main advantages of ACMI over a dry lease is that the aircraft continues operating under the lessor's AOC, letting the lessee add capacity without the time and regulatory process a new AOC would require.

Can ACMI be used for cargo and freighter capacity, not just passenger flights?

Yes. Freighter ACMI is a common way for cargo operators to add capacity during peak demand periods, such as e-commerce shipping seasons, without the cost and commitment of acquiring additional aircraft.

How quickly can an ACMI wet lease be arranged?

Because the aircraft operates under the lessor's existing AOC rather than requiring new certification, ACMI arrangements can typically be put in place considerably faster than a dry lease — often within days for genuinely urgent capacity needs, depending on aircraft availability.
