ACMI & Wet Lease

ACMI Costs Explained: What Airlines Actually Pay

Published October 22, 2026 · 10 min read

TL;DR

ACMI wet lease pricing is quoted per flight hour — not per block hour — and bundles Aircraft, Crew, Maintenance, and Insurance, but not fuel, ground handling, or overflight permits. Typical 2026 rates: ATR 72 turboprops $4,500–$7,000 per flight hour; A320-200 and 737-800 $8,000–$14,000 per flight hour; A330-200/300 widebodies $15,000–$22,000 per flight hour; 777 widebodies $25,000–$35,000 per flight hour. Peak seasons — Hajj, summer, AOG surge — push rates 20–50% above baseline. Short-term contracts under 3 months carry a 15–30% premium over 12-month terms.

Wet-lease pricing is the least transparent corner of commercial aviation. Published rate cards rarely exist, quotes vary by operator on the same route, and the underlying cost structure — what the ACMI rate actually pays for — is often misunderstood by buyers comparing it to a dry lease or an ad-hoc charter. This guide breaks down what an ACMI rate covers, what it does not, and the six variables that move the number 30% in either direction on the same aircraft.

What does ACMI stand for — and what does an airline actually pay for?

ACMI stands for Aircraft, Crew, Maintenance, and Insurance — the four cost centres bundled into a single per-flight-hour rate. Each of the four contributes a materially different share of the total, and understanding that split is what lets a buyer interrogate a quote intelligently.

  • Aircraft — the dry-lease equivalent of the airframe and engines, amortised over the contract term. For a narrowbody this typically represents 35–45% of the ACMI rate; for a widebody, 40–50%.
  • Crew — cockpit and cabin, with the operator maintaining type ratings, recurrent training, and reserve crews. Roughly 20–30% of the rate on a narrowbody.
  • Maintenance — line maintenance, component support, A-check reserves, and heavy-check provisioning. Typically 20–25% of the rate.
  • Insurance — hull, third-party liability, passenger liability, and war-risk cover for the operating region. Around 5–10% of the rate, rising sharply for high-risk areas.

That structure is what separates ACMI from a dry lease. A dry lease gives the lessee only the aircraft — the crew, maintenance, and insurance sit with the lessee, who brings them under its own AOC. A dry lease looks cheaper on the headline number, but once crew payroll, maintenance reserves, and insurance premiums are added back, the total cost of a dry lease usually lands within 15% of an ACMI on equivalent utilisation.

Where most buyers get burned is assuming the ACMI rate is the whole cost. It is not. See our ACMI & wet lease services for the full operational scope — the cost side is only half the picture.

Is ACMI priced per block hour or per flight hour?

ACMI is priced per flight hour — wheels up to wheels down. Ad-hoc charters are usually priced per block hour — engines on to engines off — which includes taxi time. This distinction is not cosmetic: it is the single most common source of quote confusion.

On a typical short-haul sector, taxi-out at origin and taxi-in at destination add 10 to 15 minutes to block time beyond flight time. On a 90-minute flight, that is a 15–20% difference in effective cost per flight hour. If you are comparing a per-flight-hour ACMI quote against a per-block-hour charter quote without normalising, you are comparing two different things.

The convention is not universal. Some jurisdictions and some larger operators quote "wet hour" — which in most contracts equals flight hour. A few operators with older contracts still use block hour. Always confirm the metric on the quote — it is a one-line question that avoids a five-figure misunderstanding.

What costs are not included in the ACMI rate?

Six cost categories sit outside the ACMI rate and are paid by the lessee:

  • Fuel — the largest single variable. An A320 burns roughly 2.4 t/hr; an A330 roughly 5.5 t/hr; a 777 roughly 7.5 t/hr. At $850/t, that is about $2,000/hr for the A320 and about $4,700/hr for the A330 — comparable to or exceeding the ACMI rate itself on narrowbodies.
  • Airport fees — landing, parking, and air navigation charges, invoiced per movement.
  • Ground handling — turnaround services, baggage, catering, cleaning, de-icing in winter.
  • Overflight permits — each country on the routing charges separately, and multi-country corridors accumulate quickly.
  • Crew per diems and hotel — usually pass-through unless explicitly bundled, and material on long-haul ACMI deployments.
  • Positioning and ferrying — a one-off ACMI usually absorbs one ferry leg; longer-term contracts rarely do. Short-term wet leases frequently price ferry legs separately.

Two more variables worth watching: war-risk insurance top-ups for regions the hull underwriter classes as elevated risk (charged to the lessee in most contracts), and diversion costs when an unplanned alternate landing is required. On a 12-month wet lease these are predictable and small; on a single-season deployment they can move the effective rate 5–10%.

How does seasonality change ACMI pricing?

Three seasonal multipliers move ACMI pricing, and each is priced differently because the demand is priced differently.

  • Hajj season (Dhul Hijjah, May–June) — +30% to +50% on widebody rates. Widebody demand concentrates in the same six-week window across every region, and the available A330 and 777 capacity is finite.
  • Summer peak (July–August) — +15% to +25% on narrowbodies, more on widebodies. Driven by European and Gulf leisure traffic, plus North American regional demand.
  • AOG surge (unscheduled) — +20% to +40%. When an operator needs an aircraft within 48–72 hours, they are buying availability, not price.

Booking six to nine months ahead is the single most effective mitigation — it moves the buyer out of the spot market and into the forward schedule, where operators price at committed-utilisation levels rather than peak-demand levels. Why do ACMI rates vary by region and contract length?

Region determines the operator pool and the positioning cost; contract length determines how much of that cost the operator amortises into the rate. Both move the number substantially on the same aircraft type.

By region, on equivalent aircraft:

  • Europe — highest baseline. Mature market, tight regulatory overhead, and the highest crew cost base.
  • Middle East — competitive on widebody because of large hub carriers with surplus capacity between seasons; narrower margin on narrowbody.
  • Asia — variable. Cheaper on turboprops and narrowbody; more expensive on widebody, where demand exceeds regional supply.
  • Africa — highest relative premium. Fewer ACMI providers, longer positioning distances, and permit complexity all add cost.

By contract length, on the same aircraft and route: 1–3 month terms carry a 15–30% premium over baseline; 6–12 month terms typically land at baseline; 24+ month terms earn a 10–20% discount because positioning cost is amortised and utilisation is committed. The pricing curve is steepest at the short end — a 30-day wet lease can cost 40% more per flight hour than the same aircraft on a 24-month term.

Why route ACMI through a broker instead of going direct?

A broker returns options in 2–4 hours versus 24–48 hours direct, works across 20+ operators rather than one, and standardises protective contract clauses. The trade-off is a broker margin — typically 3–8% on top of the operator rate.

Four advantages matter most during peak-season or AOG deployments:

  1. Speed of sourcing. When three operators are queried in parallel, the fastest viable response is returned within hours rather than the next business day.
  2. Compliance vetting. Brokers pre-verify AOC standing, EU/US carrier status for regulatory access, and insurance limits — the three failure modes that surface late in a direct process.
  3. Multi-aircraft capability. A peak programme requiring four aircraft across three months is substantially easier to close through a broker than through direct negotiation with four operators.
  4. Follow-on support. AOG swaps and mid-contract substitutions are handled inside the broker relationship — the broker has alternates ready because it sourced the original aircraft.

Brokers do not always win on cost. A flag carrier with existing operator relationships and in-house contract capability will often get a better number direct. For first-time buyers, regional operators without procurement teams, or anyone sourcing on a compressed timeline, the broker's 3–8% margin usually saves more than it costs.
