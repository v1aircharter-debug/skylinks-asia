What Documents Do You Need for an International Charter Flight?

By the Skylinks Asia Charter Desk · Reviewed by our Operations Team · Published October 2026

TL;DR: An international charter needs permission from every country it flies over or lands in — not just its origin and destination. The two core permit types are overflight permits (to cross a country's airspace) and landing permits (to touch down there), each backed by a standard document set: AOC, Certificate of Airworthiness, Certificate of Registration, insurance certificate, and often a noise certificate, plus the flight schedule, crew details, and passenger or cargo manifest. Lead times vary enormously by country — some process overflight permits in days, others take over a week for a charter landing permit. This is exactly why permit planning must start well before the flight date, not once the aircraft is already booked.

Why does every country require its own permission?

The legal foundation here is the 1944 Chicago Convention, which established ICAO and confirmed that every state has complete sovereignty over the airspace above its territory. Contracting states generally permit overflight by other states' aircraft, but in practice, almost every civil aviation authority requires prior approval before an aircraft enters its airspace or lands — there is no global blanket permission, even among Chicago Convention signatories.

Four distinct permit types cover most situations:

  • Overflight permit — authorization to enter, cross, and exit a country's sovereign airspace without landing.
  • Landing permit — authorization to land at a specific airport, confirming there's no safety, noise, or (for revenue flights) commercial objection to the operation.
  • Special permit — required when an aircraft isn't operating on a normal certificate of airworthiness, typically for ferry or delivery flights, and usually needs separate Ministry of Transport approval.
  • Diplomatic permit — required for government or military aircraft, issued by the destination country's Ministry of Foreign Affairs rather than its civil aviation authority. See our repatriation and government charter guide for how this applies specifically to government and diplomatic missions.

What documents does almost every country require?

While exact requirements vary by country, the following set covers what most civil aviation authorities ask for on a commercial charter permit application:

  • Air Operator's Certificate (AOC) — required for commercial/charter operations (private, non-revenue flights are often exempt from this specific requirement)
  • Certificate of Airworthiness (COA)
  • Certificate of Registration (COR)
  • Certificate of Insurance (COI)
  • Noise certificate, where applicable to the aircraft type and country
  • Complete flight schedule, including entry and exit points and the intended ATC route
  • Crew details, often including license copies and current medical certificates
  • Passenger details (for passenger charters) or consignee and consignor details (for cargo charters)
  • Dangerous goods documentation, where relevant — see our DGR guide for Pakistan for what that documentation actually involves

An ICAO flight plan must also be filed for the flight itself, with the approved overflight or landing permit number entered in Item 18 of that plan — a detail that matters because a missing or incorrect permit number at this stage can delay or block departure even when the underlying permit was properly granted.

How long do permits take to process?

This is where international charter planning gets genuinely country-specific, and where delays most often creep in. India, for example, publishes typical processing timeframes of around 3 days for an overflight permit, 7 days for a private landing permit, and 10 days for a charter landing permit. Other countries move faster for simple overflight-only routings; some, particularly smaller island states, don't require an overflight permit at all, only a landing permit if the aircraft actually touches down. A few jurisdictions require a local agent to submit the application rather than accepting it directly from the operator or broker.

The practical implication: international routings, especially multi-country ones, need permit lead time built into the planning timeline well before the charter itself is booked — not as an afterthought once the aircraft and crew are confirmed. If you're unsure whether your broker is handling this properly, our article on how to vet a charter broker covers the verification questions worth asking.

What about customs and ground clearance?

Separate from overflight and landing permits, the Chicago Convention also addresses customs: a country can require that a flight land at a specifically designated customs airport, and aircraft are generally admitted temporarily free of duty for the flight itself, subject to that country's own customs regulations. In practice, this means customs and immigration clearance at the airport of landing is a separate step from the permit process — cargo charters in particular need consignee and consignor details finalized ahead of arrival so that customs clearance doesn't become the bottleneck after a smoothly approved flight.
