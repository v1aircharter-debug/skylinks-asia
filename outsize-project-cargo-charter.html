Outsize & Project Cargo Charter: Moving What Won’t Fit on a Standard Freighter

By the Skylinks Asia Charter Desk · Reviewed by our Operations Team · October 2026

TL;DR: Project cargo is large-scale equipment — power transformers, wind turbine blades, mining machinery, satellites, oil and gas modules — that is too heavy, too long, or too wide for a standard freighter’s door or floor loading. It moves on specialist aircraft purpose-built for it: the Antonov An-124 (up to 120 tonnes, nose- and rear-ramp loading, onboard crane), the Ilyushin IL-76 (around 50 tonnes, rugged rear-ramp loading into secondary airports), and nose-loading widebody freighters like the Boeing 747-8F for long single pieces that fit standard width but exceed standard length. Because the global fleet of true outsize aircraft is small, project cargo charter needs to be booked earlier than standard cargo charter — sometimes weeks ahead, not days.

What is the difference between heavy-lift and outsize cargo?

The two terms get used interchangeably, but they describe different constraints, and the distinction matters for choosing the right aircraft:

  • Heavy-lift is a weight problem. A piece of equipment is within normal dimensions but simply too heavy for a standard freighter’s floor loading or payload limit — a generator set or an armored vehicle, for example.
  • Outsize is a dimensional problem. A piece might not be especially heavy, but its length, height, or width won’t pass through a standard freighter’s cargo door or fit on its main deck — a wind turbine blade or an assembled satellite, for example.

Many genuine project cargo shipments are both at once, which is exactly why aircraft like the An-124 exist: they are built to solve both problems simultaneously, with a cargo bay long and wide enough for outsize dimensions and a floor and ramp rated for heavy-lift weights.

Which aircraft are used for outsize and project cargo charter?

Four aircraft types cover the vast majority of outsize project cargo missions. Each solves a different constraint, and the choice comes down to which dimension is driving the shipment:

The An-124’s onboard crane is a genuinely distinguishing feature: it means cargo can be loaded or unloaded even at a destination airport with no heavy ground-handling equipment of its own, which matters enormously for mining sites and remote energy infrastructure across the regions Skylinks serves.

One practical constraint worth planning around: the global fleet of true An-124s is small, and the Russian-operated portion of that fleet has been largely grounded or stranded by sanctions since 2022 — the Ukrainian-operated Antonov Airlines fleet remains the main commercially active source. That scarcity is a real driver of the longer booking lead times project cargo charter typically needs compared to standard cargo charter. How does cargo actually get loaded onto an An-124 or IL-76?

Project cargo does not get palletized and rolled on the way standard freight does. Three loading methods cover almost everything:

  1. Nose-loading (747-8F, An-124): the entire nose of the aircraft swings or tilts up, letting long items be driven or winched straight down the main deck without having to turn a corner inside the fuselage — essential for anything close to the bay’s maximum length.
  2. Rear-ramp loading (IL-76, An-124): a ramp at the tail allows wheeled or tracked cargo to be driven straight on and off — the standard method for vehicles, mobile equipment, and drive-on machinery.
  3. Onboard cranes and winches: aircraft like the An-124 carry their own lifting equipment, which matters when the item is not self-propelled, or when the destination airport has no heavy-lift ground equipment of its own.

Loading a genuinely outsize piece is slow, deliberate work — a real-world example from one operator’s recent project involved a 100-tonne mobile crane loaded at roughly two feet per minute, with the crane’s top clearing the fuselage ceiling by mere inches, and the loading process alone taking around five hours. This is normal for the category, not a sign of a problem — it is part of why project cargo charter is planned well ahead of the flight itself rather than booked reactively.

What industries actually use project cargo charter?

Project cargo charter serves a consistent set of industries, each with its own recurring cargo profile:

  • Mining — haul trucks, drill rigs, crusher components, often moving to remote sites with limited surface transport access. See our piece on remote airstrip operations for African mining.
  • Oil and gas — blowout preventers (BOPs), pumps, pipeline sections, and modular processing units; see our oil & gas equipment charter guide for the aircraft-category distinctions specific to that sector.
  • Power generation — turbines, transformers, generator sets, where a failed component can mean a plant offline until the replacement arrives.
  • Renewable energy — wind turbine blades and nacelles, which are outsize by length even when not especially heavy; relevant to the wind corridors developing across Pakistan’s Sindh coast as much as anywhere else in the region.
  • Aerospace — satellites, engines, and airframe structures, almost always time-critical against a launch or maintenance window.
  • Defence and government — vehicles, equipment, and materiel moving under charter rather than scheduled freight for both capability and scheduling reasons.
  • Rail and infrastructure — large rolling stock and structural components for projects that can’t wait on ground transport timelines.

Humanitarian airlift operations often overlap with the same aircraft pool when relief cargo is outsize or when infrastructure at the destination can’t take a standard freighter — see our humanitarian relief charter guide for that angle.

What should you have ready before booking an outsize charter?

Because project cargo charter involves more variables than a standard charter, a few things are worth confirming early rather than at the last minute. Having these ready when you approach a broker collapses the lead time significantly:

  • Exact dimensions and weight of each individual piece, not just the total shipment
  • Whether the item is self-propelled or needs winching / craning
  • Runway length and strength rating (PCN) at both origin and destination
  • Ground-handling equipment availability at the destination — especially if the aircraft itself doesn’t carry an onboard crane
  • Any dangerous goods classification (some outsize items ship under DGR — see our dangerous goods charter guide)
