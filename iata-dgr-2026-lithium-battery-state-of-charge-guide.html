IATA DGR 2026: What Changed for Lithium Battery Shippers

The 30% state of charge limit is no longer a recommendation for lithium-ion batteries packed with equipment. From 1 January 2026, it is a condition of carriage. Here is the operational breakdown for shippers, forwarders and charter buyers.

By the Skylinks Asia Charter Desk · Reviewed by our Operations Team · Published October 2026

TL;DR: IATA DGR 2026 (67th Edition) extended the mandatory 30% state of charge (SoC) limit to lithium-ion batteries packed with equipment under PI 966, including Section II shipments above 2.7 Wh. Standalone lithium-ion batteries (UN3480) remain Cargo Aircraft Only. Shippers must update packing procedures, train staff and verify the DGD before tendering cargo. Non-compliance penalties can exceed $50,000 per violation.

What changed in IATA DGR 2026 for lithium batteries?

The 67th Edition of the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations, effective 1 January 2026, made the 30% state of charge limit mandatory for lithium-ion batteries packed with equipment under Packing Instruction 966. Previously, this was a recommendation. The change applies to Section I shipments and to Section II shipments where cells or batteries exceed 2.7 Wh. A higher state of charge is only permitted with approval under Special Provision A331 from the State of Origin and the State of Operator.

The change is operational, not cosmetic. A battery packed at 50% SoC under the old recommended practice is now non-compliant unless it carries the A331 approval. Consignments prepared under 2025 procedures are already being rejected at acceptance in some markets.

What is the 30% state of charge rule and which shipments does it apply to?

State of charge is the remaining energy in a battery as a percentage of its rated capacity. The 30% limit applies to lithium-ion cells and batteries offered for air transport. The table below sets out the 2026 position by packing instruction.

Packing Instruction UN Number Configuration 2026 SoC requirement
PI 965 UN3480 Batteries alone Mandatory 30% or less; Cargo Aircraft Only
PI 966 UN3481 Packed with equipment Mandatory 30% or less (Section I and Section II above 2.7 Wh)
PI 967 UN3481 Contained in equipment Not mandatory, but IATA recommends 30% or less

The practical effect is that a laptop with an installed battery is treated differently from a spare laptop battery in the same box. The installed battery falls under PI 967. The spare falls under PI 966 and must meet the 30% limit.

UN3480 vs UN3481 vs UN3090/3091 — how do shippers classify correctly?

Classification starts with the chemistry and the configuration. Lithium-ion batteries are UN3480 when shipped alone and UN3481 when packed with or contained in equipment. Lithium metal batteries follow the parallel sequence: UN3090 alone, UN3091 with or contained in equipment. Sodium-ion batteries now sit under UN3551 and UN3552.

Two classification errors recur in rejected shipments. First, shippers treat a spare battery as part of the device it powers. Second, shippers apply lithium-ion rules to lithium-metal batteries. Both produce a DGD that does not match the package, and acceptance staff are trained to catch the mismatch.

Watt-hour calculation is the other gate. Watt-hours equal voltage multiplied by amp-hours. A 3.7 V, 2.5 Ah cell is 9.25 Wh. A 11.1 V, 5 Ah laptop pack is 55.5 Wh. The Wh rating determines whether the shipment falls under Section IA, IB or II, and whether it can move on passenger aircraft at all.

When is Cargo Aircraft Only (CAO) mandatory?

Standalone lithium-ion batteries (UN3480) and standalone lithium-metal batteries (UN3090) are forbidden on passenger aircraft. They move as Cargo Aircraft Only. The package must carry the CAO label, and the air waybill must reflect the restriction.

For batteries packed with equipment, the CAO requirement depends on the Wh rating and the section. Section IA and IB shipments of UN3481 are generally CAO. Section II shipments may move on passenger aircraft within quantity limits, provided the 30% SoC limit is met where applicable.

The distinction matters for charter planning. A passenger-configured freighter or a combi aircraft cannot carry CAO-only cargo in the passenger cabin. The charter must be a dedicated freighter or a passenger aircraft with the cargo in a certified main-deck or lower-deck hold, and the operator must hold the correct dangerous goods approval.

What documents does a DGR charter shipment require?

Every DGR air shipment requires three layers of communication: marking on the package, hazard labels, and documentation. For a charter, the documentation pack typically includes:

  • Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods (DGD): the IATA form certifying classification, packing, quantity and compliance. It must be signed by trained personnel.
  • Air waybill: marked with the Dangerous Goods notation and the CAO status where applicable.
  • Packing list: showing UN numbers, proper shipping names, net quantity and package count.
  • UN-certified packaging: bearing the UN specification mark, with the correct hazard diamond and lithium battery handling label.
  • Supporting approvals: A331 approval if the state of charge exceeds 30%, or any state-of-origin or state-of-operator exemption.

For a charter, the broker should receive the DGD before the aircraft is committed. A DGD that arrives after the aircraft is on the ground is a commercial risk, not just a compliance risk. The operator may refuse the cargo, and the charterer still owes the ferry and standby costs.

How does a charter broker vet a DGR shipment before booking?

A broker does not replace the shipper's legal responsibility, but a broker that understands DGR prevents expensive mistakes. The vetting sequence is straightforward. First, confirm the UN number and packing instruction from the shipper's DGD. Second, confirm the aircraft type and operator hold the correct dangerous goods approval for the commodity and the configuration. Third, confirm the route states accept the commodity and the CAO status. Fourth, confirm the ground handler at origin and destination can receive and store DGR cargo.

The common failure is a broker who books a passenger aircraft for a shipment that requires CAO, or a freighter that cannot physically take the package dimensions. Both are avoidable with a DGD review before the charter is confirmed.

What are the penalties for non-compliance?

Civil penalties for DGR violations can exceed $50,000 per violation in the United States and the European Union. Beyond fines, non-compliant cargo can be rejected, offloaded or seized. Repeated violations can affect a shipper's ability to tender cargo with certain carriers. In a charter context, a rejected shipment can also trigger aircraft standby charges, crew duty limits and re-planning costs that dwarf the original freight rate.

The compliance burden is not hypothetical. IATA estimates that more than 1.25 million dangerous goods shipments move by air annually, and lithium batteries are the fastest-growing category. Regulators have responded by tightening the rules every year since 2016.
