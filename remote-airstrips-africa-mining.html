Top 5 Remote Airstrips in Africa for Mining

Updated 8 November 2026 · Skylinks Asia Team

TL;DR: Heavy mining equipment cannot reach most African extraction sites by road, so the industry depends on air charter into remote dirt airstrips. Commercial freighters cannot operate from 1,200 to 1,600 metre unpaved strips. Instead, ramp-loading aircraft such as the Ilyushin Il-76TD (40 to 48 tonnes, 20-wheel landing gear) and Antonov An-12 (18 to 20 tonnes) carry excavators, drill rigs and generators directly to site. Every mission begins with a California Bearing Ratio check to verify soil strength, followed by overflight and landing permits that take 24 hours to two weeks. Costs typically run $28,000 to $45,000 per block hour for an Il-76 and $12,000 to $18,000 for an An-12. Kolwezi (FKZW), Koidu-Sefadu (GFKA), Lubumbashi (FZQA), Moanda (FOON) and Tsumeb (FYTU) are among the most active mining airstrips.

Africa holds roughly 30% of global mineral reserves, but road and rail links to the extraction sites are frequently absent. A 30-tonne excavator cannot travel overland to a mine that has no road, so the industry uses air charter to bridge the gap. The aircraft involved are unusual: rear-ramp loaders designed for unpaved runways, operated into 1,200 to 1,600 metre dirt or gravel strips. This guide covers the aircraft, the physics of a dirt runway, the airstrips involved and the operational sequence for a successful mining charter. For a broader view, see our urgent cargo charter service and cargo charter in Africa.

Why can't commercial freighters land at African mining airstrips?

Commercial Boeing and Airbus freighters need 2,500 to 3,000 metres of paved concrete. Most African mining airstrips are 1,200 to 1,600 metres of compacted red dirt, gravel or laterite. The wheels sink, the pavement rating is absent, and the aircraft cannot be certified for the strip. Ramp-loading turboprops and rugged freighters are the only viable answer, and they also carry their own loading equipment in the form of a rear cargo ramp.

Which aircraft are used for remote African mining charters?

Aircraft selection follows three constraints: payload, runway surface and loading equipment. The table below covers the aircraft most frequently chartered for African mining work.

Aircraft Payload Typical role Runway
Antonov An-12 18 to 20 tonnes Drill rigs, generators, camp supplies Unpaved, 1,500 m
Ilyushin Il-76TD 40 to 48 tonnes Excavators, haul trucks, mine equipment Semi-prepared, 1,800 m
Lockheed L-100 Hercules 20 to 23 tonnes Short bush strips, STOL missions Unpaved, 1,000 m
Boeing 737-800BCF 22 to 23 tonnes Hub feeder, paved strips only Paved, 2,000 m

What is the California Bearing Ratio and why does it matter?

The California Bearing Ratio (CBR) measures soil strength. Before committing an aircraft, the broker must verify that the strip's CBR supports the aircraft's footprint load. A 70-tonne Il-76 landing on soft ground can sink to the axles, triggering a multi-million dollar recovery and weeks of equipment downtime. Wet-season ground is especially hazardous, and some strips are only usable in the dry season.

How does dust affect remote strip operations?

Prop wash during the dry season creates dust storms that reduce visibility to zero on landing roll and damage turbine engines. Turboprops are preferred because they are more tolerant of dust ingestion than pure jets. Operators frequently water the strip before arrival and time landings for early morning when the air is calm. Cargo is often offloaded in the shortest possible window, with handling crews standing clear of the propeller arc.

Which African airstrips serve mining logistics?

The five airstrips below are the most active semi-public mining strips on the continent. Private client strips are not listed for reasons of confidentiality, but they follow the same operating profile.

  1. Kolwezi (FKZW), DRC: The Katanga Copperbelt's main entry point for cobalt and copper equipment. Daily Il-76 operations are routine.
  2. Koidu-Sefadu (GFKA), Sierra Leone: Diamond mining capital of West Africa. Heavy seasonal rains, so precise weather planning and STOL aircraft are required.
  3. Lubumbashi (FZQA), DRC: A larger international gateway serving the entire Copperbelt. Heavy cargo lands from Europe or Asia and transships to An-12 or Il-76 for the final hop.
  4. Moanda (FOON), Gabon: Serves some of the world's largest manganese reserves. Remote plateau geography requires airlift of chemicals and replacement parts.
  5. Tsumeb (FYTU), Namibia: Serves copper, zinc and lead mines in the arid Namibian desert. High-altitude density and dry conditions demand careful payload calculations.

How is fuel handled at remote African airstrips?

Local Jet A1 supply is often absent or unreliable at remote mining strips. The standard practice is to fly fuel drums in on the same aircraft or a separate support flight, then pump manually into the aircraft. Fuel planning is frequently the largest constraint on a mining charter: it determines whether the aircraft must return to a paved base between loads or can remain on site.

For oil and gas fields the same logic applies. Our oil and gas air cargo service handles rig moves and crew rotations into comparable strips.

What permits are required for African mining charters?

Every cross-border charter needs overflight and landing permits, and lead times vary from 24 hours to two weeks. Cabotage rules restrict foreign operators moving cargo between two points inside the same country. Diplomatic clearance is required for certain cargo. The practical sequence is:

  1. Confirm payload, equipment dimensions and destination strip CBR and length.
  2. Secure overflight and landing permits for each sovereign airspace.
  3. Verify operator AOC, insurance and strip-specific approvals.
  4. Confirm fuel, ground handling and loading equipment at destination.
  5. Execute with 24/7 flight-following and dust mitigation planning.

Why work with a broker for remote African airstrips?

Orchestrating a heavy cargo charter into a remote African strip is not a point-and-click operation. It requires a broker who knows the CBR of the strip, the turnaround time for ground equipment, the fuel situation and the permit authorities. A single wrong CBR assumption can turn a $200,000 mission into a $2 million recovery.

Skylinks Asia verifies runway data, sources licensed AOC holders rated for unpaved strips and coordinates fuel logistics where local supply is compromised. We are a charter broker, not a carrier.
