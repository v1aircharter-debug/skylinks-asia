War-Risk Insurance and High-Risk-Zone Charter

What changes and what to ask before you book

By the Skylinks Asia Charter Desk · Published November 2026 · Figures reflect reported market conditions at publication

TL;DR: Aviation war-risk insurance is a separate layer of cover protecting an aircraft against war, missile strikes, terrorism, and similar perils, which standard aviation policies exclude. When a conflict flares, insurers reprice it quickly. Since late February 2026, war-risk premiums for aviation across the Gulf and wider West Asia have risen by roughly 50% to 500%, and reported per-flight additional premiums for foreign carriers have run from about $50,000 for a charter trip to $70,000–$150,000 for scheduled carriers flying into the region. For a charter client, that cost typically appears as a per-flight or per-landing surcharge in the quote, and it can change between the quote and the flight date. The practical protection is to ask the right questions before you book: who holds war-risk cover for the destination and alternates, who bears the additional premium, and what happens if airspace closes after you have committed.

What is war-risk insurance, and why is it separate from standard cover?

Standard aviation hull and liability policies exclude war and allied perils. Cover for those risks — war, hijacking, terrorism, missile and drone strikes, confiscation — is bought separately as war-risk insurance, usually as an extension or a standalone policy. Skylinks Asia arranges charter across regions where this distinction matters routinely.

Two features matter for anyone chartering into a volatile region:

  • It reprices fast. War-risk underwriters assess risk by geography. Insurers can reprice cover or cancel it on short notice (seven days is the commonly cited standard), and flights into areas the insurer has designated as high-risk generally need an additional premium agreed before departure.
  • It is tied to specific airspace and airports. Cover is quoted against the destination, the routing, and the alternate airports. A flight that is perfectly insurable into one country can be uninsurable or prohibitively priced into the next.

What did 2026 do to the Gulf war-risk market?

The scale of the 2026 repricing is the reason this topic belongs on a charter site now. Following the escalation of the regional conflict at the end of February 2026 and the airspace closures that followed across the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait, Lockton's aviation specialists reported war-risk premiums for aviation operating across Gulf and West Asian airspace rising by 50% to 500%, with insurers reassessing exposure to airspace interdiction and concentrated risk at key transit hubs.

The per-flight numbers reported in the press show how that lands in practice:

  • Foreign scheduled carriers were quoted an additional $70,000 to $150,000 per flight into the region, enough to make some routes uneconomic. Ten international airlines suspended flights to much of the Gulf, and Dubai Airports' chief executive publicly urged foreign governments to help reduce insurance costs so carriers could resume service.
  • Charter war-risk insurance to the Middle East was reported at roughly $50,000 per trip in March 2026, alongside sharply higher and more volatile fuel costs.
  • Market commentary through autumn 2026 indicates some Gulf hubs are slowly returning to insurers' maps, while individual cities can move back into elevated-risk territory after new incidents. The picture remains uneven rather than settled.

These are reported ranges, not tariffs. The actual figure for a given flight depends on the aircraft, the routing, the airports, the dates, and the day the quote is requested.

How does war-risk cost show up in your charter quote?

For a client, war-risk cost usually arrives in one of three ways:

  1. A separate per-flight or per-landing line, or an additional-premium (AP) line, added to the base mission price. This is the cleanest form because you can see exactly what is war-related.
  2. Folded into the mission price, which is simpler to read but makes it harder to see whether you are being charged for risk that does not apply to your route.
  3. A quote with a short validity window, because the premium itself can change before the flight. In a volatile period, expect shorter validity than the usual charter quote, and expect the operator to reserve the right to adjust if the underwriter reprices before departure.

This is the same mission-pricing logic covered in our charter cost guide, with one extra moving part that did not exist at this scale on these routes a year ago.

What should you ask before you book a flight into an elevated-risk region?

For any charter touching an elevated-risk region, confirm the following in writing:

  • That war-risk cover is in place for the destination, the routing, and the planned alternate airports, and that it is held by the operator operating your flight. This extends the verification steps in our broker-vetting guide: ask for evidence of cover, not just a statement that it exists.
  • Who bears the additional premium, whether it is fixed or can be adjusted before departure, and by how much.
  • What happens if airspace closes or cover is withdrawn after booking: whether the flight can be rerouted to an alternate airport, whether the quote reprices, and what the cancellation terms are.
  • Which alternates are planned, since an alternate that sits in a separately restricted area can undercut the whole plan.
  • Whether permits and clearances are current for the actual routing — see our international charter documents guide in Related Reading for the document set involved.
  • What your own cover includes. If you are shipping cargo, war risks are typically handled under separate cargo-insurance war clauses, often applying only while the goods are aboard the aircraft. Confirm with your cargo insurer rather than assuming the charter's cover extends to your goods.

Regional authorities also publish conflict-zone information bulletins that operators and brokers monitor. A broker who tracks these and can explain how they apply to your routing is doing the work you are paying for.

Can charter still operate when scheduled airlines suspend service?

When scheduled airlines suspend service because the insurance math no longer works, charter operations can sometimes continue, since a single dedicated mission can be underwritten and priced individually in a way a full schedule cannot. That flexibility is part of why charter demand surged in the early weeks of the 2026 disruption.

It is the same principle behind the diplomatic and repatriation work covered in our government and repatriation charter service, and behind the cargo missions flown on aircraft such as the Boeing 777, which offers the payload and range needed for long-haul relief charters.

It comes at a price, and it depends on the operator being able to secure cover and the relevant authorities allowing the operation.
