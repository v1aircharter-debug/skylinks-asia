Quick Answer

Dangerous goods (DGR) charter moves cargo that scheduled passenger airlines often can't or won't carry — explosives, flammable chemicals, corrosives, and Class 9 items like lithium-ion batteries — using IATA-certified freighters, trained handling staff, and the specific documentation (a Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods and Air Waybill) every DG shipment requires.

Regulatory Rigor

What Makes Dangerous Goods Charter Different?

Every DG shipment carries requirements standard freight doesn't, regardless of aircraft type.

Formal Documentation

A Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods (DGD) and a properly completed Air Waybill are required for most classes, specifying the UN number, proper shipping name, hazard class, and packing group.

UN-Specification Packaging

Packaging must carry UN specification markings and pass performance testing — stacking, vibration, drop, and pressure — appropriate to the hazard class and quantity being shipped.

Certified, Recurrently Trained Personnel

IATA mandates recurrent dangerous goods training for handling staff on a fixed cycle, not a one-time certification — a detail worth confirming with any operator or handler before booking.

Operator & Aircraft Approval

Not every cargo operator is approved to carry every DG class. A charter broker's job includes confirming the specific aircraft and operator hold approval for your cargo's classification, not just general cargo capability.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is IATA DGR and why does it matter for charter?

The IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations (DGR) is the global standard governing how hazardous materials are classified, packaged, documented, and handled for air transport. Charter operators and ground handlers must be specifically approved to carry the DG class in question — general cargo capability isn't enough.

What documents are required to charter dangerous goods by air?

Most DG shipments require a Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods (DGD) and a properly completed Air Waybill, specifying the UN number, proper shipping name, hazard class, and packing group. Some smaller, limited-quantity shipments have reduced documentation requirements.

Why do commercial passenger airlines refuse certain dangerous goods shipments?

Passenger airlines carry strict quantity and packaging limits for DG cargo, and some classes or quantities are barred from passenger aircraft entirely for safety reasons, regardless of how well they're packaged. A dedicated cargo charter often remains the only legal option for these shipments.

What's the difference between DG charter in general and Cargo Aircraft Only (CAO) shipping?

DG charter covers any hazardous shipment moved by dedicated freighter. CAO is a specific designation for shipments that are legally barred from passenger aircraft but still permitted on cargo aircraft under stricter packaging and labeling rules — see our detailed CAO guide for which classes this applies to.

Can Class 1 explosives be shipped by air charter?

Some can, under strict conditions — most explosives divisions are either forbidden outright or restricted to Cargo Aircraft Only, with specialized security, segregation, and often government authorization required. Only a narrow subset is permitted on passenger aircraft at all.

How is dangerous goods cargo packaged for air transport?

Packaging must carry UN specification markings and meet performance standards — drop, stacking, vibration, and pressure testing — appropriate to the hazard class, packing group, and quantity. Combination packaging with inner receptacles and outer packaging is common for chemicals and liquids.

How far in advance should I book a dangerous goods charter?

Standard DG shipments typically need a few days' lead time to confirm operator approval, permits, and ground handling for the specific hazard class. Genuinely urgent DG shipments can be prioritized — see our urgent cargo charter page for how emergency dispatch works.

Do dangerous goods charter operators need special certification?

Yes. Operators and ground handling staff must hold specific approvals for the DG classes they carry, and IATA requires recurrent dangerous goods training for handling personnel on a fixed cycle rather than a one-time qualification.
